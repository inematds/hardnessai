MODULE 3.5 · TEACHING MODE

🔬 Grill-me & adversarial skills

There's a skill of 4-5 sentences that Matt Pocock calls "unreasonably effective" — absurdly effective. It turns AI into a adversarial interviewer that questions you about your plan until the two of you arrive at a shared understanding. In this module, you’ll understand why this replaces “plan mode” and get the exact text to copy.

📖 Living glossary (the NEW terms in this module)

Track 1 has already established model, prompt, agent, skill, codebase e harness. Here are the terms specific to grill-me. Come back whenever you need to:

grill-me — a short skill that asks AI to "grill" (grill = question/challenge): it becomes an interviewer and asks questions about your plan, one at a time, until you agree on everything.
adversarial — an approach where the AI disagrees on purpose: points out gaps, cases you forgot, and alternatives instead of just agreeing with you (the famous “yes-man” who says yes to everything).
plan mode — a multi-agent mode (e.g., Claude Code) where the AI first writes a plan and only runs after you approve. Grill-me is an alternative to this.
shared understanding — "shared understanding": the state where you and the AI see the problem the same way, with no hidden misunderstandings, before a line of code is written.
design tree — "design decision tree": the set of choices in a project, where one decision depends on another (branches). grill-me goes through them branch by branch.
PRD — Product Requirements Document, product requirements document: the text that describes what will be built. It’s usually the result a good grill-me session (you’ll see this in 3.6).
1

🔥 What grill-me does

🧠 Imagine it this way: you have to defend an idea in front of a panel. Instead of someone who smiles and approves everything, a demanding interviewer sits across from you and asks, "what if the user does X?", "why not use Y?", "what happens when this fails?" You leave the room with a stronger idea — because the gaps showed up beforehand, not afterward. grill-me turns AI into exactly that interviewer.

A grill-me is a skill that does just one thing: transforms the model in a adversarial interviewer. Instead of you giving an order and the AI obeying, the relationship is reversed: it asks, you answer. It asks questions, raises ideas you hadn’t considered, points out dependencies between decisions — and keeps going until the two of you reach a shared understanding about what will be done.

Pocock describes this skill as "unreasonably effective" — absurdly effective for something so small. The reason is simple: modern models tend to be too agreeable. If you say "my idea is X," AI tends to praise it and move forward, even if X has a huge hole. grill-me deliberately breaks that habit: it tells AI to disagree, find what’s missing, and challenge you. Common mistake: thinking this is just "the AI asking questions." The difference is in the stance — adversarial, not sycophantic — and the discipline of asking one question at a time (you'll see why in topic 3).

The normal flow: you ask, the AI does. grill-me reverses it: the AI asks, you clarify.

⚠️ Common beginner mistake

Confuse grill-me with "cute brainstorming mode." If the AI is agreeing with everything you say, the skill isn't working. The entire value comes from the adversarial stance: it has to challenge you, not flatter you.

In one sentence: grill-me = the AI becomes an adversarial interviewer and questions you about the plan until you agree on everything.

Going deeper (optional): why "grill"?

In English, to grill someone is slang for "to interrogate firmly" — like a detective or reporter who won’t let go. The word comes from "grilling": keeping someone "over the fire" until the truth comes out. The skill’s name deliberately carries that intent: this isn’t a pleasant conversation, but a productive interrogation of your own plan.

2

🔄 Replace plan mode

🧠 Imagine it this way: two ways to plan a trip. In the first, the agency gives you a ready-made itinerary and you just say "okay"—but if it misunderstood your destination, you only find out at the airport. In the second, the agency interview before (“beach or mountains? traveling with a child? what’s the budget?”) and the itinerary is created already aligned. The grill-me is the second approach.

Many agents have a plan mode: the AI reads your request, writes a plan, and waits for you to approve it before touching the codebase. It’s useful, but it has a flaw: the plan is a monologue. The AI guesses what you meant and spits out a document. If it misunderstood one detail at the beginning, the whole plan goes off track — and you only notice when you reread everything.

Pocock uses grill-me in place of plan mode. His phrase sums it up well: "here's my idea, interview me, let's reach shared understanding, flush out weirdness before we implement" — here’s my idea; interview me, let’s reach a shared understanding, let’s tease out the oddities before implementing. The key difference: plan mode is AI assuming; grill-me is the AI asking. Instead of a plan that might be wrong, you build understanding together through dialogue. The reason is that misunderstandings are expensive: a wrong assumption becomes wrong code, which becomes rework.

Quick retrieval: what is the key difference between plan mode and grill-me?

In one sentence: Plan mode is the AI assuming the plan on its own; grill-me is you building understanding through dialogue before coding.

3

✂️ Short and powerful (4-5 sentences)

🧠 Imagine it this way: A good instruction from a boss isn’t 10 pages long. It’s three precise lines: “Call each customer, one at a time, and before each call, tell me what you recommend.” That’s what grill-me is like — tiny, but every sentence carries a behavior rule that changes everything.

The grill-me fits in 4–5 sentences. Every sentence is a deliberate rule — it’s not filler text, it’s dense instruction. Notice how each part does a job: "interview me relentlessly... until we reach a shared understanding" defines the goal (keep interviewing until there’s alignment). "Walk down each branch of the design tree, resolving dependencies between decisions one-by-one" gives the method: go through the design tree branch by branch. "For each question, provide your recommended answer" requires AI not only to ask, but also to recommend her answer (to give you a starting point).

The last two sentences are the secret sauce. "Ask the questions one at a time" — one question at a time. Without this, AI dumps 12 questions on you at once and you get lost; one at a time keeps you focused and lets each answer shape the next question. And "if a question can be answered by exploring the codebase, explore the codebase instead" — if the AI can answer by looking at the code, it looks at the code instead of asking you. This keeps it from grilling you about things it can figure out on its own. The common mistake here, “improving” the skill means bloating the text: the more you add, the more diluted the behavior gets. The strength lies in being concise. Below, the text exact to copy:

grill-me.md — the exact skill text 
Interview me relentlessly about every aspect of this plan until we reach a shared understanding. Walk down each branch of the design tree, resolving dependencies between decisions one-by-one. For each question, provide your recommended answer. Ask the questions one at a time. If a question can be answered by exploring the codebase, explore the codebase instead.

Free translation: "Interview me relentlessly about every aspect of this plan until we reach a shared understanding. Walk down each branch of the decision tree, resolving dependencies between decisions one by one. For each question, give your recommended answer. Ask the questions one at a time. If a question can be answered by exploring the codebase, explore the codebase instead of asking."

Five phrases, five rules. Purple = the ones that change behavior the most.

In one sentence: there are 4-5 sentences, each one a rule — don't inflate the text; the strength is in being concise.

4

🎯 Align before coding

🧠 Imagine it this way: an experienced carpenter measures twice and cuts once. The cost of measuring is minutes; the cost of cutting wrong is a wasted board and hours of rework. grill-me is your tape measure before the cut: you spend a few minutes answering questions so you don't waste an hour on the wrong code.

The main benefit of grill-me is moving alignment to before of implementation. Pocock's phrase— "flush out weirdness before we implement" — it’s literally this: squeezing out the quirks before of implementing. Every misunderstood decision that turns into code is expensive: the AI writes it, you review it, realize it wasn't quite right, and ask it to redo it. Each of these loops burns time and tokens. The grill-me cuts these loops off at the root, because understanding is already settled by the time coding begins.

There’s a valuable side effect: by answering the questions, you, too finds holes in your own reasoning. We often think we have a clear idea, but it only becomes clear when someone asks, “What about this case?” The grill-me forces you to think about the edge cases you skipped. That’s why it’s useful even when AI no will write the code in the end—just going through the interview improves your plan.

🔬 Worked example: grill-me on an “export report” feature

You tell the AI: "I want to add a button to export a report as a PDF" and activates grill-me. See the interview (one question at a time, with a recommendation):

  1. AI: "Should the PDF be generated on the server or in the browser?" I recommend on the server, to control the source and not depend on the user’s browser.” — You: “server.”
  2. AI: "And when the report is empty—disabled button or PDF with a warning?" I recommend disabling, it’s clearer.” — You: “PDF with a notice; the client wants a record.”
  3. AI: "I saw in the codebase that you already use the lib" pdfkit in /reports. Reuse it? I recommend reusing." — You: “yes.” (it explored the code instead of asking from scratch)

Result: in 3 questions, you settled server-side generation, empty-state behavior, and library reuse—three decisions that, if discovered midway through coding, would have meant rework.

Without grill-me, the error only shows up at the end and leads to a rework cycle; with it, alignment happens before the code.

In one sentence: a few minutes of interviewing at the start save hours of wrong code later.

5

🤝 Shared understanding

🧠 Imagine it this way: two musicians tuning up before a show. It’s not the show yet—it’s the moment when they both play the same note until they sound in sync. Only after they’re tuned do they play. “Shared understanding” is that tuning between you and the AI.

The grill-me destination has a name: shared understanding. It’s the state where you and the AI see the problem the same way—with no hidden assumptions waiting to blow up. The interview doesn’t end when the AI gets tired; it ends when understanding converges. That’s why the skill says "until we reach a shared understanding": the stopping criterion is alignment, not a fixed number of questions.

David (Pocock’s colleague in the transcript) has a widely used variation on the same principle: "describe my vision, list out the 10 most consequential decisions (software design / architectural / product) that will shape this project, and interview me until you understand 98%." — describe my vision, list the 10 most consequential decisions that will shape the project, and interview me until you understand 98%. Notice the engineering: forcing AI to list the 10 most important decisions ensures the interview focuses on what matters, not trivial details; and the "98%" gives you a concrete convergence goal. It’s in the same family as grill-me—adversarial, in dialogue, until alignment. You’ll see this complete prompt, ready to copy, in the module 5.2 of the Solutions track.

In one sentence: the stopping criterion isn’t the number of questions—it’s whether you both see the same thing.

6

🛠️ Adapt it for you

🧠 Imagine it this way: a basic bread recipe. It works for everyone, but each baker adjusts the salt, hydration, and baking time for their oven. The grill-me is the basic recipe—copy it, use it for a week, and only then season it your way.

The grill-me is a starting point, not a dogma. Since it’s just a skill of text, you can save it in your project and adapt it. Remember the golden rule from topic 3: adjust sparingly, because adding too much dilutes it. A few adjustments that fit without ruining the effect: ask for a summary of understanding at the end (“when we’re done, write a paragraph about what we agreed on”); limit the scope (“focus only on architecture decisions”); or set a cap (“maximum of 8 questions”). But start by copying the exact text—build your intuition before changing anything.

It’s worth remembering the philosophy of Pocock that runs throughout the whole track: he prefers procedures and want to be in the driver’s seat— "I know my skills, I don't want to delegate my thinking" (I know my skills; I don’t want to delegate my thinking). grill-me fits this perfectly: it doesn’t take you out of the driver’s seat; it sharpens your command. And it's the first link in a chain you'll build in the next module: grill-me → PRD → issues. Instead of dumping everything at once, you use grill-me to align, turn that alignment into a PRD, and the PRD into tasks—with you always in the driver’s seat.

Going deeper (optional): grill-me vs. "superpowers" (model in control)

In the transcript, Pocock contrasts his style with that of the “superpowers” project (Obra), which prefers the opposite: the model in control, with the AI deciding the path. Pocock is at the far end of the human in control: hides most of the AI’s descriptions and keeps the knowledge with the human. Grill-me expresses that choice—the AI asks, but you decide. There’s no universal “right” here; it’s a preference about who you want holding the wheel. As a beginner, starting with the human in control usually teaches you more.
